ChunkAct: Action Chunking for Agentic Robotics
Authors: Bingxin Xu, Wenrui Bao, Xinxin Liu, Jiawei Zhou, Yuzhang Shang, Emilio Ferrara
First: 2026-10-13T17:59:57+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-13T17:59:57+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Agentic robotics places a large language model (LLM) agent in the robot control loop, where it composes perception, motion, and vision-language-action (VLA) tools under the guidance of a memory and thereby generalizes to new tasks without retraining. This generality comes at a steep price in efficiency. Every primitive requires a round of LLM reasoning over the full interaction history, so the cost of an episode grows with the number of primitives. We find this reasoning largely redundant: once a sub-goal is chosen, its primitives follow a routine already prescribed by memory. We present ChunkAct, which introduces action chunking to agentic robotics. Online, a single call launches a chunk of chained primitives, which executes autonomously and returns control only upon failure. Offline, ChunkAct compiles the memory into these chunks, so execution is memory-free at test time and the planner is reserved for decisions that require reasoning. LLM computation therefore scales with the number of decisions rather than the number of primitives. On LIBERO-Pro, ChunkAct improves task success by 5.0 points over the state of the art in agentic robotics while requiring 2.9x fewer LLM calls, 4.4x fewer input tokens, and 2.3x fewer output tokens.

Abstract
Scaling expert demonstrations alone does not enable generalist robot policies to improve from deployment. Real-world rollouts contain successes, suboptimal behavior, and failures: imitating every recorded action can reinforce poor behavior, while filtering out failures discards evidence of physical interactions and their consequences. We introduce \textbf{LoopWAM}, a closed-loop world-action model that learns directly from this full quality spectrum, using rollouts from both itself and other deployed policies. Source-dependent noise sampling unifies behavior learning and action-conditioned world modeling, while quality steering and quality-weighted supervision retain full video supervision across qualities and regulate action imitation. Video and action tokens interact throughout parallel denoising, allowing learned consequences to inform action generation directly. On RoboChallenge Table30-V2, three deployment-and-training rounds raise success from 23.67\% to 43.33\%, a 19.7-percentage-point gain, with a final score of 56.80. LoopWAM also achieves 94.5\% average success on RoboTwin~2.0 and 49.5\% overall success on RoboCasa365 without additional embodied pretraining, obtaining the best overall results among the compared methods across all three benchmarks. On challenging shirt folding task, two rounds of deployment-driven improvement after single-task post-training raise success from 13.20\% to 55.56\%. These results demonstrate closed-loop improvement from mixed-quality experience under both multi-task learning and single-task post-training. \href{https://loop-wam.github.io/}{\textcolor{orange}{Project Page}}

EgoExplorer: A Vision-Motion-Touch Observatory for Human Loco-Manipulation In Situ
Authors: Yunsong Zhou, Xuekun Jiang, Haiqi Shen, Hangxu Liu, Chaojian Shi, Yujie Liu, Jingyuan Li, Haoran Yang, Jiafei Cao, Dongzhan Zhou, Dong Wang, Yao Mu, Chunhua Shen, Weinan Zhang

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Learning humanoid loco-manipulation from human demonstrations rests on observing how locomotion, posture, and object interaction evolve together as tasks unfold. Scaling collection across diverse environments, in turn, favors lightweight, portable capture systems. Yet, existing datasets provide only partial views of this coordination. Thus, we introduce EgoExplorer, a 100-hour vision-motion-touch observatory of human loco-manipulation built with a wearable system. With an emphasis on professional activities, its continuous recordings align egocentric video with reconstructed whole-body motion, fine-grained finger articulation, and tactile observations, accompanied by step-level descriptions. To achieve this coverage with sparse body sensing, we develop the Calibrate-Complete-Constrain pipeline. Concretely, reusable calibration fixes participant geometry and sensor attachments before motion inference to disentangle mounting offsets from articulation. A temporal prior then completes unobserved joint motion, while layered constraints promote measurement agreement, temporal continuity, and geometric consistency, properties that motion plausibility alone does not guarantee. Against tracker-fitted references, the method reduces body-point error by 71.9% relative to the strongest baseline; matched evaluations also show gains in hand stability and contact estimation. Preliminary studies explore multimodal generation and conditioning alongside humanoid retargeting with whole-body control in simulation.

Abstract
Sparse-to-dense motion completion recovers dense mesh motion from sparse vertex displacements. Residual priors trained on one geometry family frequently fail to beat parameter-free harmonic extension off-family. Moreover, cross-family training meets a scaling barrier: residual amplitude varies by more than an order of magnitude across geometry families and collapses as observations become denser. Since this scale depends on unknown target motion, it cannot be measured directly at test time. We decouple residual structure from physical scale by learning a dimensionless prior, estimating amplitude from observables and a lightweight correction fitted only on training sources. Our unified model surpasses harmonic extension across all evaluated families, including unseen cloth and legged bodies, without skeletons, registration, or cross-mesh correspondence. The same prior applies without retraining to point-tracked animal video and a robot grasping a soft body. Project page: https://su-terry.github.io/cepheid-website/

What Matters in Quality-Aware VLA Pretraining: A Benchmark and Empirical Study

Abstract
How should demonstration quality be used during vision-language-action (VLA) pretraining to improve performance after target-task fine-tuning? We compare weighting, filtering, and conditioning across reward definitions and label granularities using 5,000 naturally suboptimal human demonstrations and 24 target tasks. Within each VLA, we match demonstrations and training budgets. Additional comparisons examine pretraining data size and the roles of quality tags during fine-tuning and inference. Across three VLAs, we find that approach-aware episode conditioning improves mean success over plain behavioral cloning; conditioning and filtering favor episode and chunk labels, respectively, on average, with model-dependent reversals; and constant-tag fine-tuning improves mean success after conditioned pretraining with either tagged or untagged inference. We introduce AXIS-Bench to support these controlled comparisons. Built on crowdsourced simulation demonstrations, it provides recorded object and contact states for recomputing quality labels on shared trajectories. Replayable trajectories support workspace-aligned rendering and user-defined real-world target tasks. In our real-world experiment, simulation pretraining raises success from 9.6\% to 31.7\%. Tev: Scaling Long-Horizon Agentic Environments with Dense Progress Rewards
Authors: Wenbo Hu, Shraman Pramanick, Krishna Kumar Singh, Nanyun Peng, Kai-Wei Chang, Yong Jae Lee

Abstract
Long-horizon agents have drawn growing attention for their ability to work autonomously -- from hours to days -- on economically valuable tasks, a capability essential to automated discovery and recursive self-improvement (RSI). Yet open-source models still struggle on long-horizon tasks, largely for lack of both training data and a working recipe, and scaling both is hard: long, complex tasks are difficult to curate and verify, and their rewards are sparse. We address both. First, we introduce Tev-Gym, the first long-horizon agent training dataset with verifiable, dense, progressive rewards -- 1,170 verified task environments across 7 domains. It is built on a latent-world-first approach that first constructs a latent world: a complete and correct hidden specification of the task domain, workflow, and expected state after each step. Each task is then progressively rendered from this specification into an agent-facing environment, enabling controllable complexity and a natural progress metric. Second, we turn this metric into a training recipe: via progress-potential reward shaping (PPRS), we convert progress into a dense per-step reward that improves training efficiency and makes otherwise-intractable tasks trainable. Training with this recipe yields Tev-27B, which achieves substantial gains on the Agents' Last Exam computing and math sciences domains (ALE-CM), matching GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5 at Pass@5. Despite training only on ALE-related data, it generalizes to the Terminal-Bench (TB) 3.0 and 4.0 subsets, surpassing GLM 5.2 and Gemini 3.7 Flash, and reaches Claude Opus 4.8-level performance on middle-horizon tasks such as TB 2.1.

Abstract
Pixel-space diffusion models provide an appealing alternative to latent-space diffusion models by directly modeling raw images without relying on a learned tokenizer. However, scaling pixel-space models has predominantly followed dense strategies, tightly coupling model capacity with active computation. In this work, we show that standard LLM-style Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures can be effectively transferred to pixel-space diffusion. Abstract
Persistent homology summarizes the topological evolution of a graph as persistence diagrams (PDs). Generating graphs whose topology matches a target descriptor is increasingly important, yet existing topology-aware generative models rely on soft regularization, producing graphs whose PDs may only be approximately similar. We initiate a principled investigation of the generation problem: produce graphs that realize a target PD exactly. We propose two approaches guided by the implicit local and global constraints a target PD imposes. The local approach builds degree-based PD-equivalent graphs iteratively, and we prove that every output realizes the target PD and that every degree-based PD-equivalent graph is reachable. However, as shown thereafter, local generation necessarily trades off between sample diversity and reaching infeasible states. We therefore recast generation as a global constraint satisfaction problem with a complete encoding solvable by modern constraint programming solvers. This is applicable to any vertex-based permutation equivariant filtration scheme and is minimal under the degree filtration. Experiments on four standard graph generation benchmarks confirm that both methods realize the target PD exactly. Together, our methods provide a principled treatment of strict topology-compliant graph generation. Code is available at https://github.com/Aalto-QuML/ExactCompliance.

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) relies on costly model-generated responses, motivating larger rollout batches and square-root learning-rate scaling. Reported to approximately preserve progress per sample with one optimizer step per rollout, this rule is less understood in practical multi-step training. We develop a two-clock framework that separates the samples needed to make progress from those consumed before collapse. Under a local quadratic model, the square-root rule preserves first-order drift per sample but not progress: larger batches take fewer, longer updates and lose more to curvature, yielding a learning-rate-dependent critical batch beyond which sample efficiency degrades. A drift-threshold hypothesis predicts a complementary failure clock whose sample count remains approximately invariant along the square-root rule. Controlled GRPO experiments on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B across a $16\times$ batch range support both predictions. Ratio clipping delays failure, especially for larger batches, but the progress gap persists without it. Widening the clip trades stability for a transient speed gain. The progress gap reappears on another dataset, a general-purpose checkpoint, and a 7B model. Finally, we use the two-clock framework to derive a simple multi-step RLVR schedule: start below the critical batch and switch to a larger batch before failure. Our code is available at: https://github.com/WinstonLiyt/two-clocks-rlvr.

Abstract
Reconstructing complete, high-quality meshes of large-scale scenes from unposed multi-view images requires faithful geometric grounding while plausibly completing surfaces that are unobserved or only sparsely captured. We present WorldRecon, a generative multi-view reconstruction framework for recovering complete, large-scale 3D scene meshes from unposed RGB images. WorldRecon uses a coarse, noisy global point cloud and camera poses estimated from the input images by a reconstruction model as a global scaffold for whole-scene generation and partitions it into overlapping spatial blocks. Any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a verifier, where the harness defines what the agent may use and the verifier defines what counts as success. Every EO task can be made verifiable, through labeled benchmarks, independent sensors, physical models or the repeated observation of the same place. Abstract

Abstract
Robot policies are expected to generalize across deployment environments, including variations in the robot itself: cameras are bumped and remounted, calibrations drift, controllers run at different rates, and end-effector pads wear down. We present LIBERO-RIG, a benchmark that perturbs only the robot's hardware and setup while holding every LIBERO task fixed. The benchmark spans eleven perturbation axes, including camera mounting and optics, camera-to-arm calibration, actuation gain, control rate, proprioceptive noise, base pose, table height, and gripper pads. Each axis has five severity levels, yielding 210 conditions across the four LIBERO suites. We evaluate nine recent vision-language-action models using 500 episodes per condition and substantiate each finding with rigorous statistical tests. Pooled success falls from 0.932 to 0.767 at the middle severity and 0.509 at the highest severity. Performance degradation varies substantially across perturbation axes and action interfaces, and training on visual perturbations does not mitigate faults that leave camera images unchanged. We release the benchmark together with manifests specifying the fixed perturbation parameters for each episode.

Abstract
Robots are increasingly autonomous in task execution, yet their improvement remains largely human-driven. This study investigates Physical Recursive Self-Improvement (Physical RSI), in which physical experience guides lasting updates to both a robot's capabilities and its improvement procedure, making subsequent improvement more effective across tasks. Physical RSI faces challenges from noisy observations, continuous dynamics, costly trials, and irreversible consequences. We identify research priorities in reliable feedback, causal attribution, safety preservation, and benchmarking, outlining a path toward robots that autonomously improve how they learn while ensuring safety. Abstract
Visual reasoning is an important capability for vision-language models (VLMs) to understand visual evidence, spatial structure, constraints, and state changes. Existing evaluations mainly rely on real-world data or procedurally generated tasks. Real-world data preserve natural complexity, while procedural games provide controllable generation and verifiable solutions. However, it remains unclear whether abilities measured in toy games correspond to real-world visual reasoning and whether game examples can support transfer. We introduce Game2Real-Bench, a benchmark that studies these connections across four capability groups, including fine-grained perception, spatial reasoning, constraint reasoning, and sequential planning. Our benchmark, dataset, and code are available at https://github.com/QingAnLiu/Game2Real-Bench.

Abstract
With the rapid development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), evaluating their video understanding capabilities has become increasingly important. Abstract
Operator learning methods such as DeepONets and FNOs often struggle with PDE families featuring sharp interfaces, heterogeneous coefficients, and localized multiscale structures. We introduce a partition-of-unity (POU) mixture-of-experts framework for localized operator learning, in which geometry-aware gating networks produce smooth spatial partitions which blend the contributions of local expert networks. Our main contribution is HiRefPOU, a residual-style hierarchical POU architecture for DeepONets that organizes localized representations through nested parent-child partitions while preserving global continuity. We also show that the same POU principle can be incorporated into Fourier Neural Operators to introduce spatial adaptivity without modifying the underlying spectral layers. On heterogeneous Darcy and reaction-diffusion benchmarks, HiRefPOU achieves substantially lower error than global DeepONet and static POU-MoE baselines, while the broader operator-learning experiments show that the benefits of localization depend on the PDE structure and the chosen neural-operator backbone. The learned partitions are interpretable and align with interfaces and regions of rapid solution variation. These results show that explicit geometric localization can improve both accuracy and interpretability in neural operator learning.

Abstract
We introduce WorldOracle, a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate action-conditioned world models (ACWMs) against paired real-world executions. Given the same initial observation and low-level action sequence, WorldOracle asks whether a predicted rollout reproduces what happens in the real world, including physical consequences and task-state transitions. We evaluate models along four complementary dimensions: physical state correctness, interaction spatial alignment, key interaction area fidelity, and multi-view action coherence. To support this evaluation, we curate real-world training and evaluation suites that cover different interaction dynamics and embodiments. The evaluation suite contains long, multi-segment episodes with both successful and failed executions, enabling evaluation across varied action outcomes and behavior distributions. Experiments on seven representative ACWMs reveal distinct model strengths and failure modes. WorldOracle provides a systematic foundation for measuring and advancing action-conditioned world modeling for manipulation. We will release the datasets, evaluation code, and leaderboard.

Abstract
Complex image editing requires faithful task interpretation and accurate execution, but a failed edit does not reveal which responsibility failed. We present DuplexBridge, which uses a unified model's own understanding capability to enhance prompts for its own generation capability, without an external understanding module. A source-grounded bridge specifies targets, final-state changes, and preservation constraints. We introduce CE-Bench, a 1,200-task benchmark measuring joint satisfaction of task-derived change and preservation requirements. Abstract
In this work, we study grokking in a simple, analytically tractable setting whose rich phenomenology allows us to disentangle the effects of initialization, signal strength, weight decay, sample size, and input dimension. These factors can produce superficially similar learning curves through distinct mechanisms, complicating their interpretation. Our analysis distinguishes early fitting through nuisance features, core feature learning, and delayed generalization. We identify sharp conditions under which grokking provably occurs and derive analytic asymptotic expressions for the lengths of the generalization plateaus. Our results also characterize regimes of early generalization and failure to reach a prescribed test accuracy target. We show that the interpolation edge constitutes a distinct dynamical regime, where vanishing spectral gaps separate core learning from generalization, helping clarify the role of proximity to interpolation in existing accounts of grokking. The resulting picture is analogous to fast and slow phases in chemical kinetics and suggests that the lazy-to-rich transition discussed in the literature may reflect a similar separation of dynamical time scales. Our theoretical results are faithfully supported by extensive experiments.

Abstract
Continual reinforcement learning must balance retention with adaptation, yet many methods still rely on \emph{single-model preservation}, committing to one evolving policy as the main reusable solution across tasks. Even when a previously successful policy is retained, it may no longer provide a reliable starting point for rapid adaptation after interference, reflecting a form of \emph{loss of plasticity} that single-policy preservation cannot address. Inspired by quality-diversity methods, we introduce \textsc{TeLAPA} (Transfer-Enabled Latent-Aligned Policy Archives), a continual RL framework that organizes behaviorally diverse policy neighborhoods into per-task archives and maintains a shared latent space so that archived policies remain comparable and reusable under non-stationary drift. This perspective shifts continual RL from retaining isolated solutions to maintaining \emph{skill-aligned neighborhoods} with competent and behaviorally related policies that support future relearning. In our MiniGrid CL setting, \textsc{TeLAPA} learns more tasks successfully, recovers competence faster on revisited tasks after interference, and retains higher performance across a sequence of tasks. Our analyses show that source-optimal policies are often not transfer-optimal, even within a local competent neighborhood, and that effective reuse depends on retaining and selecting among multiple nearby alternatives rather than collapsing them to one representative. Together, these results reframe continual RL around reusable and competent policy neighborhoods, providing a route beyond single-model preservation toward more plastic lifelong agents.

Abstract
In cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), agents must learn to coordinate their actions to achieve shared objectives. A key indicator of effective coordination is behavioral diversity, i.e., the agents' potential to enact distinct yet complementary control policies given the same set of observations. Although behavioral diversity can enable role specialization and richer joint behaviors, its appropriate level is highly context- and task-dependent and often costly to tune. We propose Adaptive Diversity Control (ADiCo), a two-timescale framework that adapts behavioral diversity online during training. On a fast timescale, Diversity Control (DiCo) regulates system diversity toward a target value. On a slower timescale, an Extremum Seeking Controller (ESC) adjusts this target using only mean episodic returns, without requiring an explicit model or analytical gradient of the diversity-performance landscape. We evaluate ADiCo on six configurations across two cooperative MARL tasks, where it maintains task-appropriate diversity levels: low diversity when homogeneous policies are beneficial and higher diversity when heterogeneous policies are required. Through this adaptation, ADiCo matches or exceeds the mean performance of DiCo (best grid-searched diversity target) and Unconstrained DiCo (diversity allowed to emerge without target regulation). These results demonstrate that incorporating classical feedback control into MARL training enables effective coordination by providing a systematic alternative to task-specific manual selection of the diversity target.

Abstract
Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) enables large language models (LLMs) to learn from multi-turn interactions with verifiable outcomes. Textual skills can improve exploration by distilling prior interactions into reusable guidance. However, existing skill-based RL approaches often either assume skill injection is beneficial or rely on indirect feedback or rollout-intensive evaluation to assess its utility. Our pilot studies show that rollout-based utility estimation for skill credit assignment is costly and unstable under limited sampling, while skill benefits vary across candidates and depend on the current policy's competence on each query. To address these challenges, we introduce SkillScaffold, a policy-adaptive training scaffold that combines low-cost skill-utility estimation with selective skill injection. By reusing outcome-verified training trajectories, SkillScaffold generates candidate skills and estimates their utility through outcome-aware likelihood comparisons, without candidate-specific environment rollouts. The resulting utility guides whether and which skill to inject, retaining skill-free execution when no candidate is estimated to help. Joint training enables the Generator to produce skills tailored to the evolving Executor. Scaffolded RL encourages skill internalization for skill-free inference. Experiments on ALFWorld, WebShop, ScienceWorld, and $τ^2$-Bench demonstrate gains over strong agentic-RL and skill-based RL baselines across both short- and long-horizon tasks. Ablations and further analyses support the importance of utility-based selection and abstention. Our code is available at https://github.com/Vaidurya00/SkillScaffold.

Abstract
Raw image intensities are a poor surrogate for similarity across images in correspondence problems. Further, while auxiliary segmentation labels can provide semantic identification and improve matching, they can assign only coarse identities to fine-grained anatomy and often cover only a subset of structures, particularly in whole-body settings. We present an approach that instead establishes correspondence using complementary representations of anatomy and demonstrate its utility for longitudinal whole-body PET/CT registration in the Learn2Reg 2026 PSMAReg Challenge. Our 3D foundation model, anatomix, is used to derive fine-grained, per-voxel semantic representations that are consistent across volumes, and we further employ off-the-shelf segmentation models to obtain coarse anatomical identities. Together, these representations drive a GPU-accelerated iterative registration solver (FireANTs) in a hybrid multistage framework: the first stage estimates alignment from dense multi-channel features, after which a second refines the deformation using both anatomix- and segmentation-based losses. Lastly, because registration should not introduce non-biological tumor volume changes across time, we develop lesion-specific component-wise volume preservation schemes. Without any task-specific training, our system won first place in the PSMAReg Challenge leaderboard. Code and interactive visualizations are available at http://www.neeldey.com/reps-for-reg.

Abstract
Frontier language-model agents can now improve robots on their own: they write control code, test it, and keep what works as skills and memories for the next task. We introduce SEAR (Self-Evolving Agentic Robotics), a framework and benchmark built around two questions. Is the agent really learning, or just trying more often? And does what it learns still help on new tasks, in a new simulator, and on a real robot? The framework runs an agent in three modes: it writes code that becomes the policy (code-as-policy), acts live in the control loop (agent-as-policy), or works as a researcher that fine-tunes a vision-language-action model (auto-research). The benchmark adds controls that separate retained experience from repeated attempts, familiar tasks, privileged simulator state, larger budgets, and luck. Across seven frontier language models, 330 simulated and five physical tasks, and over four years of agent runtime, we highlight three findings. First, the learning curve of a robotic agent depends both on the overall time budget and the task difficulty relative to model capability. Harder tasks make longer sessions worthwhile; on easier tasks, re-rolling short attempts does just as well. Second, control code and shared routines transfer across tasks and simulators, while task-specific solutions do not. On a real robot, we observe a performance gap between agent-designed practice and novel human-designed scenes. Third, different models evolve more effectively in different ways. Some do best acting live, others writing code first. And a text-only model keeps pace with a comparable multimodal one: perception helps with error assessment, but agentic reasoning and tool use can matter more. We release the benchmark and codebase to support future research.

Abstract
Video diffusion models generate visually compelling short-term sequences but often struggle to preserve scene geometry and object structure over time. In this paper, we first demonstrate that current video attention cannot identify reliable correspondences across frames, with responses dispersed across multiple regions even after recognizable scene structure has emerged. We argue that reliable cross-frame associations are essential for information exchange as scene structure forms in video generation. Thus, we introduce Geometry Attention Distillation (GAD), which transfers correspondences from a frozen geometry model's attention to video attention. Extracted from clean videos, these correspondences link the same static surface or moving object across frames. Since structure formation begins in deeper layers at higher noise and shallower layers at lower noise, we apply attention distillation at a student layer selected according to the noise timestep. After training, video attention focuses more consistently on corresponding locations. Experiments across all three video backbones demonstrate improved long-range scene consistency and dynamic temporal consistency, with geometric gains across generation lengths. Authors: Koushik Howlader, Tirtho Roy, Md Tauhidul Islam, Wei Le

Abstract
Predicting spatial gene expression from standard H&E images could extend molecular analysis to tissues without spatial transcriptomic measurements. A standard Transformer applies self-attention over spots but ignores where each spot sits in the tissue, whereas a spot's molecular state depends on tissue context at multiple spatial scales. Building on this insight, we introduce MIST, a Multi-context Spatial Transformer that augments the self-attention backbone with two context pathways applied in every layer: a coordinate-invariant local pathway that attends over spatial k-nearest-neighbor spots, and a slide pathway that broadcasts a whole-slide summary to every spot; the model predicts all spots in a single forward pass. Because coordinates enter only through pairwise distances, the local pathway is invariant to translations, rotations, and reflections when image features are fixed. In intra-cohort evaluation across six human organ cohorts from HEST-1k, MIST improves mean Pearson correlation over the strongest evaluated baseline by 9.7%, 11.6%, and 11.4% for 10-, 50-, and 100-gene panels, respectively, with the lowest average MSE and MAE. Together, these results show that adding local and slide context to a vanilla Transformer backbone is an effective approach to spatial gene expression prediction from histology.
